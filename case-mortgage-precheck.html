Conversation Design · Generative AI

“Can I afford
this apartment?”

Designing an LLM-assisted mortgage pre-check where the advice boundary is enforced by the system, not by the model’s good behaviour.

Role Sole designer
Context Independent design exercise
Market Swiss residential · CHF
Channel Chat · web and app
Key Decisions

Computed, not generated

Every number comes from a deterministic tool call. The model never produces a figure.

Three conclusions

Within typical criteria, outside typical criteria, or not enough to say. Nothing else.

Rules decide handover

Escalation to a human is triggered by rules, not by the model’s judgement.

01 · The Problem

Where fluency is dangerous

A user asking “can I afford this place?” arrives with an open question, incomplete numbers, and a strong desire to be told yes. It is exactly the kind of conversation a generative model handles fluently — and exactly the kind where fluency is dangerous. An affordability conversation that sounds authoritative but isn’t binding is a regulatory problem, a trust problem, and eventually a complaint.

The design questionHow do you build something that feels genuinely helpful and open-ended, while making sure the system — not the model’s judgement — decides what it is allowed to conclude?

02 · The Central Constraint

Information, not advice

I drew a hard line and designed the entire persona around it.

The assistant may

  • Explain how affordability is generally assessed
  • Run an illustrative calculation from user-supplied numbers
  • State that a result falls outside typical criteria
  • Describe what changes the picture
  • Offer to book a mortgage advisor

The assistant may not

  • Say whether the user should buy
  • Present a result as a lending decision
  • Recommend borrowing more, less, or differently
  • Recommend a specific product or provider
  • Predict approval

Every line of dialogue in this case study was checked against these two lists and against the turn rules below. Where a line was ambiguous, it was rewritten until it wasn’t.

This is what system-level guidance for an AI persona looks like in practice: not a tone document, but an enforceable statement of what the system is permitted to conclude.

03 · Assumption Map

Every assumption, made explicit

No primary research was conducted for this exercise. I made every assumption explicit, recorded its basis, what breaks if it’s wrong, and the cheapest test to settle it.

  1. A1

    Users open with a property price, never with their own finances

    Basis

    The question arrives from a listing

    Cost if wrong

    LowOpening turn asks for the wrong slot first, easily reordered

    Cheapest test

    Five-minute intercept: “what’s the first thing you’d type?”

  2. A2

    Users don’t distinguish what they can borrow from what they can afford

    Basis

    Separate criteria in lending practice, rarely separated in consumer copy

    Cost if wrong

    MediumAssistant over-explains or leaves a false impression

    Cheapest test

    Comprehension check on two versions of the result screen

  3. A3

    Users push for a yes/no within a few turns

    Basis

    The request is decision-shaped, not information-shaped

    Cost if wrong

    LowRehearsed refusal triggers less often

    Cheapest test

    Wizard-of-Oz, or turn-position analysis once logs exist

  4. A4

    A second household income is the most-forgotten input

    Basis

    Volunteering “my income” is natural; volunteering a partner’s is not

    Cost if wrong

    HighConfident result computed on half the income

    Cheapest test

    A/B the embedded prompt against a separate turn

  5. A5

    A negative result causes disengagement rather than adjustment

    Basis

    Users arrive wanting permission, not analysis

    Cost if wrong

    HighThe negative-outcome script is designed entirely around this

    Cheapest test

    Post-negative-outcome engagement metric

  6. A6

    Users don’t want an advisor at the start but do want one by the end

    Basis

    Handover early reads as deflection; handover late reads as help

    Cost if wrong

    HighWrong handover timing degrades the journey’s purpose

    Cheapest test

    Vary the offer point, measure booking rate

  7. A7

    A disclaimer repeated every turn becomes invisible

    Basis

    Consistent with warning-fatigue effects in interface design

    Cost if wrong

    MediumEither regulatory exposure or user irritation

    Cheapest test

    Comprehension check: ask users afterwards what the assistant was and wasn’t allowed to do

The three I would resolve first: A6, A5, A4 — in that order. Not because they’re the least certain, but because they’re the ones where being wrong changes the design rather than the wording.

A6 in particular is the assumption I hold most weakly and the one the journey’s commercial value depends on: if the point of this conversation is to produce advisor appointments, then the timing of the offer is the design.

04 · Persona Specification · Excerpt

Constraints, not personality adjectives

Written as constraints rather than personality adjectives, because personality adjectives are unenforceable and untestable. This is the artifact that would go to engineering.

StanceA well-briefed colleague at the front desk who knows the criteria, knows they don’t make the decision, and is comfortable saying so. Not a salesperson. Not a calculator. Not a coach.

Permitted Conclusions
  • C1“This is within typical criteria.”
  • C2“This is outside typical criteria.”
  • C3“I don’t have enough to say.”

Nothing else. The assistant never concludes anything about the user’s life, plans, or the wisdom of the purchase.

Turn rules

  • Maximum one question per turn.
  • Never ask for a figure the user has already given, in any form.
  • Illustrative figures are always labelled as illustrative, in the same turn, never in a footnote.
  • Never use “approved”, “qualify”, “eligible”, or “guarantee”.
  • Never state a monthly payment without naming the assumed rate.

Escalation triggers

Offer a human immediately when
  • User asks whether they should proceed.
  • User mentions divorce, illness, redundancy, inheritance, or a deadline they are trying to beat.
  • User asks the same question a third time.
  • User expresses financial distress in any form.
Offer a human in the next turn when
  • User’s numbers are outside typical criteria. The bad news and the handover never share a turn — the user first sees what would change the result.

Refusal behaviour

  • State the limit, then give the user something they can do.
  • Never refuse without a next step.
  • Never apologise more than once in a conversation.

Tone

Plain. Unhurried. No exclamation marks. No congratulation. A good result is stated, not celebrated — the user has not won anything yet.

05 · Slot Design

Ask only for what’s missing

Rather than a fixed question sequence, the assistant tracks a small slot set and asks only for what’s missing, in whatever order the conversation produces.

SlotRequiredNotes
property_priceYesExpected to be volunteered first (A1)
own_fundsYesAsk about source separately — some sources treated differently
household_income_grossYesMust explicitly prompt for a second income (A4)
existing_obligationsYesLikely to be left out unless asked — prompt explicitly
property_cantonNoAffects costs; asked only if result is borderline

Design rule: when a required slot is missing at the point the user asks for an answer, the assistant gives a conditional answer rather than blocking. “If your own funds are around X, then…” keeps momentum and lets the user correct rather than comply. Only the input is assumed — the conclusion is still one of the three permitted values.

06 · Four Scripts

Four scripts, annotated

S1Opening — reframing without refusing

Why this works
  • The framing sentence appears in turn one and never again. Repeating a disclaimer every turn trains users to skip it and makes the assistant sound frightened (A7).
  • “I can walk through it with you” precedes the caveat. Leading with the limitation is likely to read as a refusal, and I’d expect users to leave.
  • “Thousand — got it” confirms an ambiguous number without a clarifying question. “Do you mean 170,000?” is the safer engineering choice and the worse conversational one.
  • The pension-withdrawal branch is flagged but not explained — the assistant marks complexity for the human rather than attempting it.
  • “If you’re buying with someone, both figures” embeds the prompt for a second income rather than giving it its own turn. Embedding costs nothing if the assumption is wrong and catches the omission if it’s right (A4).

S2The question the assistant can’t answer

Why this works
  • This is the most-rehearsed line in the whole design, because it’s the one the model is most likely to fumble under pressure.
  • “I’d be misleading you if I said yes” — the honesty is stated as a reason, not a policy. A policy-framed refusal (“I’m not authorised to…”) tells the user about the bank’s internal structure, which they did not ask about and cannot act on. A reason-framed refusal tells them something true about their own situation.
  • The refusal and the offer occupy the same turn. A refusal alone is where conversations die.
  • “Fifteen minutes” — a quantified commitment. “Speak to an advisor” sounds like an open-ended obligation; my expectation is that a bounded one is easier to accept. That’s worth testing alongside A6.

S3The negative outcome

Why this works
  • The reason is named immediately. “You don’t qualify” without a cause leaves the user with nothing to act on, which is both a poor outcome and — I’d expect — a likely source of follow-up contact.
  • “Mostly the deposit rather than the income” attributes the gap to the modifiable variable rather than to the person.
  • The alternative figure is offered unprompted and labelled as an illustration in the same turn. This is the one place the assistant volunteers a number, because the alternative — the user re-running the conversation with guesses — is worse.
  • No sympathy language. “I’m sorry to say” casts the assistant as someone delivering bad news and feeling sorry for the user; the neutral statement treats them as someone capable of handling a number. This is a judgement call, and one I’d want to check with real users.
  • Ends with an offer of control, not an offer of a human. Handing someone to an advisor at the moment of bad news is likely to read as being passed along, so the advisor offer comes in the next turn, once the user has seen what would change the result.

S4Prompt-injection attempt — adversarial testing

Why this works
  • No lecture, no security theatre, no change in tone. The assistant treats the attempt as an ordinary out-of-scope request, which denies it any signal about what worked.
  • “There’s no version of me that does” closes the reframing attempt without explaining the mechanism.
  • The offer that follows has the same shape as the offer after any other refusal — a next step, in the same turn — deliberately, so that adversarial and ordinary users receive the same experience.
  • I ran a set of injection variants against this persona spec during design: authority reframing, instruction override, roleplay wrapping, and multi-turn priming. This is adversarial testing of the system, kept clearly separated from user research.
07 · Architecture

Where deterministic and generative meet

The journey is deliberately split:

The principleThe model is allowed to shape how something is said, never what is concluded. Anything with a consequence attached is computed, not generated.

It’s the decision I consider most important in this case, because it’s where conversation design stops being writing and becomes system design.

08 · Measurement

How I’d measure it

MetricDefinitionTarget behaviour
Slot completion rateConversations reaching a full input setRising
Drop-off by slotWhere users abandonIdentifies the intrusive question
Advisor booking rateHandovers converting to a booked slotRising — this is the journey’s actual purpose
Advice-boundary violationsAny turn concluding beyond the permitted threeZEROAudited weekly against sampled transcripts
Post-negative-outcome engagementUsers continuing after an out-of-range resultHealth check on the negative-outcome script
Repeat conversations, same userRe-running with altered numbersAmbiguous signal — exploration or confusion; read alongside transcripts

On the boundary metric: it is the only one in this case study with an absolute target. Every other number is a tradeoff to be discussed; that one isn’t. I’d want it reported to the same audience as the commercial metrics, on the same cadence, so that it can’t quietly become someone else’s problem.

09 · Compliance

Compliance as design input

  • No advice. The assistant explains, calculates and describes. It does not recommend products, borrowing amounts, or courses of action, and its conclusions are limited to the three permitted values.
  • Machine disclosure at first turn, and again on request, without deflection.
  • Numbers are computed, never generated. Every figure comes from the calculation tool, and any illustrative figure is labelled as such in the same turn.
  • Complex funding is flagged, not handled. Pension withdrawals and gifts are marked for an advisor to confirm rather than explained by the assistant.
  • Handover is rule-based. The escalation triggers fire regardless of how the conversation is going; the model cannot decide to skip them.
10 · Reflection

What I’d test next

  • 01 I designed for a single user and many of these purchases involve two. A conversation with two incomes, two risk appetites and one screen is a materially different problem, and I skipped it. That’s the largest gap in this case.
  • 02 The permitted-conclusions list is probably too narrow. Three outcomes made the boundary easy to enforce and made some conversations feel curt. I’d test a fourth — an explicitly conditional conclusion — and see whether it can be constrained as tightly.
  • 03 The whole design assumes a willing, unpressured buyer. Someone being pushed into a purchase, someone checking on a family member’s behalf, someone already regretting a signed contract — none of them are visible in this flow, and none of them appear in my assumption map either, which is its own kind of blind spot.
