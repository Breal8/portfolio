“My Card Isn't Working”

Designing a blocked-card journey for a Swiss retail bank assistant.

Not affiliated with any bank. All product names and policies are illustrative.

[01]

THE PROBLEM

Card problems are the highest-emotion, highest-volume moment in retail banking support. The user is often standing at a checkout or in another country. Three things are true at once:

  • The user wants one thing: use my money again.
  • The bank cannot say much until identity is established.
  • Getting it wrong is expensive both ways — a false block strands a customer, a false unblock enables fraud.

Nothing here is solved by better copy alone. It is solved by deciding what the assistant is allowed to do, in what order, and when it must stop.

[02]

WHAT USERS ACTUALLY THINK

I ran five think-aloud sessions (friends and colleagues, 28–61, all Swiss retail banking customers) using a Wizard-of-Oz setup where I played the assistant.

The prompt: “Your card was just declined. Open your banking app and get it sorted.”

What I observedWhat I changed
Nobody distinguished blocked, expired, frozen, or insufficient funds. They said “it's not working.”One entry point. The assistant handles branching internally.
Four of five assumed the assistant already knew which card and which transaction.Confirm known context out loud rather than asking for it.
All five became tenser when asked a security question they couldn't answer instantly.Verification steps need a “can't do that right now” exit that isn't framed as failure.
Two used the word “stolen” before they had any evidence.Treat emotive language as a signal to slow down, not as a resolved intent.

The mental model: users do not think in intents. They think in “something between me and my money is broken, and I want to know whose fault it is.”

[03]

DESIGN PRINCIPLES

  1. Resolve before you explain. State the situation, then the reason, then the options. Never open with policy.
  2. Never make the user prove they aren't a fraudster. Verification is framed as the bank checking itself, not interrogating them.
  3. One question per turn. Stress narrows working memory.
  4. Every dead end has a door. No turn may leave the user without a next action.
  5. Handover is a feature, not a failure. The assistant offers a human early and without shame.
  6. Say less when the stakes are higher. As risk rises, sentences shorten.

TWO SCRIPTS

Happy path — bank-initiated block, chat
AssistantI'm the digital assistant — I can help with cards, payments and account questions, and pass you to a colleague any time you'd like.

Your debit card ending 4417 was blocked at 14:06 today after a payment in Milan.
UserI'm in Milan. That was me.
AssistantThanks — that helps. Before I unblock it, one quick check on our side. I'll send a confirmation request to your banking app. Can you approve it there?
Userdone
AssistantConfirmed. Your card is active again — try the payment in about a minute. If it's declined again, tell me and I'll bring in a colleague straight away.
Userwhy was it blocked
AssistantTwo payments in different countries within a short window. It's an automatic check — it stops fraud, and it occasionally stops travel. You can tell us about upcoming trips in the app to make it less likely.
Why this works

Turn 1 discloses machine status and scope in one breath — users who suspect they're talking to a bot spend the next three turns testing it instead of solving their problem. The block is stated with card, time, and place before any question, converting “something is broken” into “a specific thing happened.”

Verification is framed as “one quick check on our side” — the bank's obligation, not the user's burden. The explanation is deferred until asked; users who want to pay do not want a fraud policy briefing. “It stops fraud, and it occasionally stops travel” tested better than an apology, which invited “so fix it.”

Suspected theft — the assistant slows down
Useri think someone has my card, there's a payment i didn't make
AssistantI've blocked the card now, so nothing else can go through it.
AssistantI'm bringing in a colleague — they'll go through the payment with you and start the claim.
AssistantWhile you wait: don't cancel anything yourself, and don't reply to any texts or calls about this, even ones that look like they're from us. We'll only ever contact you inside the app.
Why this works

Action first, in a turn of its own — the user's fear is ongoing loss, and the block ends it before anything else is discussed. No questions are asked; every question here is a delay, and the human will ask them anyway.

Three consecutive assistant turns — a deliberate exception to the one-turn rule. The pacing mimics urgency rather than describing it. The last turn is anti-phishing guidance; fraud victims are targeted again within hours by callers impersonating the bank. A conversation that ends without this line is incomplete.

TONE OF VOICE

DoDon'tWhy
“Your card was blocked at 14:06.”“It appears your card may have been blocked.”Hedging on facts the bank owns reads as evasion.
“I can't do that.”“Unfortunately I'm unable to assist with that request at this time.”Length signals bureaucracy.
“Try again in about a minute.”“Please allow up to 60 seconds for the change to propagate.”System language transfers the bank's problem to the user.
“A colleague will go through it with you.”“Your case has been escalated.”Escalation is a bank word; it sounds like trouble.
One tone rule for this journey

The assistant never apologises for the block itself. The block worked as intended. Apologising for a correct security action teaches users that security is an inconvenience the bank is embarrassed by.

HOW I'D MEASURE IT

The trap I'd flag

Containment rate — share of conversations without a human — is the metric most likely to be requested and most likely to damage this journey. In a fraud flow, high containment can mean the assistant is retaining conversations it should release. I'd pair it with appropriate-handover rate and refuse to report it alone.

[08]

COMPLIANCE AS DESIGN INPUT

  • No unmasked data before verification. Last-four only; balances and addresses withheld until identity is confirmed.
  • No advice. The assistant states facts and performs actions. It does not recommend products, limits, or courses of action.
  • Verification cannot be negotiated. No path where persistence, distress, or repetition produces an unverified unblock — a defence against social engineering.
  • Machine disclosure at first turn, and again on request, without deflection.
  • Fraud-report language is fixed. Designed around, not rewritten.
[09]

WHAT I'D TEST NEXT

  • Alternative verification paths. The whole journey depends on the user having their phone. I'd map what's actually available (SMS, email, voice biometrics) and design the conversation around that, rather than treating alternatives as fallbacks.
  • The “nothing is wrong” ending. When the card is healthy and the merchant terminal failed, the assistant has good news and no action — and that script is the weakest in the set. Conversations that end without an action are harder to write than ones that end with a fix.
